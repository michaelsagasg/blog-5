## Attach the log to something that already happens weekly

The most reliable way to keep a log going is attaching it to an action that's already happening anyway, the weekly injection itself. Writing the entry immediately after the injection, before moving on to anything else, means the log doesn't need its own separate reminder or motivation, it simply rides along with a routine that's already established for other reasons.

### Why standalone reminders tend to fail

A phone reminder or a calendar note for "update log" is easy to dismiss or snooze once the novelty of a new habit wears off, usually somewhere around week three or four. Tying the log to an existing physical action removes this failure point almost entirely.

## Keep the entry short enough to sustain

A log that takes thirty seconds to fill in each week is far more likely to survive past the first month than one that asks for detailed notes, calorie counts, or long reflections every time. Four or five short fields, dose, site, weight, a brief note, is enough to be useful without becoming a chore.

### Expanding later, once the habit is established

Adding more detail, a rotation chart, food notes, side effect tracking, is easier to introduce once the core habit of writing something down every week is already solid, rather than trying to start with an elaborate format from day one.

## Review the log periodically, not just fill it in

A log that's only ever written into and never looked back on loses a lot of its value, and the lack of any payoff can make it feel pointless to keep up. Setting a simple habit, reviewing the last month's entries every four weeks, gives a concrete reason to keep filling it in beyond the first few weeks of initial motivation.

### What a monthly review looks like

Scanning back through the past four entries, noting the overall direction rather than any single week, and jotting a one-line summary at the end of the month turns the log from a pile of numbers into something that actually gets used.

## Plan for the weeks that get missed

An occasional missed week is normal over a multi-month course, and treating a gap as a reason to abandon the log entirely is a bigger setback than the missed entry itself. Simply picking the log back up the following week, without trying to reconstruct the missing data, keeps the habit alive.

## Signs the habit is starting to slip

Skipping the log entry immediately after an injection and telling yourself it'll be filled in later that day is an early warning sign worth noticing, since "later" often becomes "never" once a few days pass. Catching this pattern in the first week or two it happens, rather than after a month of gaps has already built up, makes it much easier to course-correct.

### A small reset if the log has already lapsed

If a log has already gone quiet for a few weeks, the easiest way back in isn't trying to reconstruct the missing weeks from memory, it's simply starting a fresh entry this week and continuing forward. A gap in the middle of an otherwise long-running log is a minor cosmetic issue, not a reason to abandon the whole record.

## Enlisting a small amount of accountability

Sharing a brief weekly summary with a partner, friend, or an online community, even just a sentence, can add a small amount of external accountability that makes a habit more likely to stick through a slow month than relying on internal motivation alone. This is entirely optional, and a private log kept only for personal use works just as well for anyone who prefers it that way.

### A final practical note

The first month is the hardest part. Once a log has survived past week four, the habit tends to become considerably easier to sustain for the months that follow.

## The bottom line

A log survives past the first month when it's tied to an existing routine, kept short enough to fill in quickly, reviewed periodically for a sense of payoff, and treated as resumable rather than ruined by an occasional missed week.
