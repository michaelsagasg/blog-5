## Timing affects the number more than people expect

Body weight naturally varies across a single day, generally lowest first thing in the morning before eating or drinking, and higher later in the day after meals and fluids. This swing can be a kilo or more, which means weighing in at different times across different weeks introduces a variable that has nothing to do with any real change, but shows up in the log exactly as if it did.

### Morning weigh-ins tend to be the most consistent

Weighing first thing in the morning, after using the bathroom and before eating or drinking, removes most of the daily variation caused by food and fluid intake, which is why this is the most commonly recommended timing for a weekly log.

## Same day, same conditions

Beyond time of day, weighing on the same day each week, and under similar conditions, similar clothing or none, the same scale, the same location, removes further sources of variation that have nothing to do with an actual trend. Switching between a bathroom scale at home and a different scale elsewhere, even occasionally, can introduce a jump that looks like a real change but isn't.

### What to do when a normal routine isn't possible

If travel or a change in routine means the usual conditions aren't available for a particular week, noting that in the log, "weighed in the evening this week, different scale," explains an outlier entry rather than leaving it to look like an unexplained jump in the trend.

## Why this matters more for a weekly log than it seems

A weekly log with inconsistent timing effectively has two variables changing at once each week, the actual trend and the noise introduced by different conditions, which makes the two much harder to separate. A log with consistent timing has only the trend and the normal biological fluctuation to account for, which is a much clearer signal to read.

### The payoff over several months

Early on, the difference between a consistent and inconsistent weigh-in routine might not seem to matter much. Over three or four months of entries, a consistently timed log produces a noticeably smoother, more readable trend line than one where timing varied from week to week.

## A simple routine that locks in consistency

Pairing the weigh-in with something else that already happens at a fixed time each morning, right after using the bathroom and before breakfast, for instance, turns consistent timing into an automatic habit rather than something that needs to be remembered and decided on separately each week. This kind of pairing is the same principle that makes injection logging itself easier to sustain: attach the new habit to an existing one rather than relying on memory alone.

### What to do on an unavoidably different week

Illness, travel, or an unusually early or late start to the day will occasionally disrupt the usual routine. Weighing in under whatever conditions are actually possible that week, and simply noting the deviation in the log, is better than skipping the entry altogether, since a noted outlier is far easier to account for later than a gap in the record.

## A quick comparison of consistent versus inconsistent logs

A log kept with consistent timing across 12 weeks typically shows a reasonably smooth trend line with only the expected small bumps from normal fluctuation. The same underlying trend, logged with inconsistent timing, morning some weeks, evening others, can look considerably more erratic on the page, even though the actual change happening in the body is identical. The difference is entirely in how the data was collected, not in what actually happened.

### A final practical note

This is one of the simplest, lowest-cost changes that improves a log's readability, requiring nothing more than picking a time and sticking to it.

### One last reminder

Small, boring habits like a fixed weigh-in time rarely feel important in the moment, but they're often exactly what separates a genuinely useful log from a confusing one months later.

## The bottom line

Weighing in at a consistent time, on a consistent day, under similar conditions, removes a substantial source of noise from a weekly log, making the actual trend far easier to read accurately than frequency alone ever could.
