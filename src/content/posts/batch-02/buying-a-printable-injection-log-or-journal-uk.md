## A printed journal or a free template both work

There's no functional difference between a purchased injection log journal and a free printable template, as long as both include the same core fields: date, dose, injection site, and weight. UK stationery retailers and online marketplaces sell dedicated dosing journals from around £8 to £15, often with a weekly grid already laid out. A free printable template, found through a quick search and printed at home, does exactly the same job for the cost of paper and ink.

### What a bought journal adds

A purchased journal usually comes pre-bound, which suits someone who wants something durable enough to last a full course without pages falling out. Some also include extra sections for side effects, measurements, or notes, which saves designing that layout from scratch.

## What fields actually matter

Whether bought or printed, a useful weekly entry needs at minimum: the date, the dose in mg or units, the injection site used, and the current weight. Space for a short note, how the week felt, any side effects, anything unusual, turns a bare number log into something that's actually useful to look back on months later.

### Printing your own for free

A simple table with those columns, printed and put in a ring binder, works just as well as a bought journal for most people. This is the cheapest option and suits anyone who wants to adjust the layout as they go, adding a column for injection site rotation or side effects partway through a course without needing a new journal.

## Digital alternatives

A spreadsheet or a tracking app does the same job without needing physical storage, and makes it easier to build a chart from the weight column later. The choice between paper and digital usually comes down to which one a person will actually keep up with consistently, since the best format is the one that gets used every week, not the one with the most features.

### Combining paper and digital

Some people keep a quick paper note on injection day, then transfer it into a spreadsheet or app once a week. This adds a small amount of extra effort but catches transcription errors early and keeps a physical backup in case a phone or laptop is unavailable.

## Buying in the UK

Large stationery chains and online retailers both stock dosing or fitness journals suitable for this purpose, though few are specifically branded for GLP-1 logging. A generic weekly planner or habit tracker journal with a grid layout works just as well as one marketed specifically for injections, often for a lower price.

## Making a printed template last a full course

A log printed on standard paper can wear out or smudge over a multi-month course, particularly if it's handled daily. Printing on slightly heavier paper, or keeping pages in plastic sleeves inside a ring binder, extends its life considerably for a minimal extra cost. Numbering each week's page in advance, one to twelve for example, also makes it obvious immediately if a week has been skipped.

### Backing up a paper log

A quick photo of each completed page, taken with a phone and saved to a dedicated album, is a simple backup against a lost or damaged notebook. This takes seconds and means a physical log doesn't represent a single point of failure for months of tracked data.

### A note on handwriting versus typing

Some people find handwriting a log entry, even briefly, makes the habit feel more deliberate and is more likely to stick than typing into an app. This is a personal preference rather than a rule, but it's worth trying a paper format for at least the first few weeks if a digital habit hasn't worked well in the past.

## A short checklist before choosing

Decide between paper and digital based on which is likelier to be kept up weekly. If buying a journal, check it has space for date, dose, site and weight at minimum. If printing a free template, choose one with room to add columns later, since a log that starts too narrow often needs reworking a few weeks in. Whatever the format, setting it up and having the first page ready before the first dose is due means week one starts with a complete entry rather than a gap to fill in later.
