## A flat week is statistically normal, not a warning sign

Across any multi-week log, it's common and expected to see at least one week where the number on the scale doesn't move, even while the overall multi-month trend continues in the expected direction. This happens because week-to-week change is small relative to the normal noise in a single weigh-in, so a real change can easily be masked by unrelated fluctuation in any given week.

### What "the noise" is made of

Water retention from sodium or carbohydrate intake, digestive contents at the time of weighing, sleep quality, and for some people hormonal cycles, can each shift a reading by a kilo or more in either direction. A genuine weekly change from fat loss alone is often smaller than this range, so a flat or even slightly up week doesn't necessarily mean progress has stopped.

## Distinguishing a flat week from a genuine plateau

A single flat week is not the same as a plateau. A plateau is a pattern that shows up across several consecutive weeks with no movement in the underlying trend, not one isolated entry. Reviewing four or more weeks together, rather than reacting to any single week, is the only reliable way to tell the difference.

### How a log makes this distinction possible

Without a written record, it's difficult to remember whether a stretch of flat weeks was actually four in a row or just felt that way because the most recent one stood out. A log removes this guesswork by showing the actual sequence of numbers side by side.

## What to check during a flat week

Reviewing that week's other log entries, sleep, stress, any change in routine, a different weigh-in time or scale, often explains a flat reading without anything needing to change about the underlying course. Context recorded in the log at the time is far more reliable than trying to reconstruct it from memory afterwards.

### When a flat stretch is worth a closer look

If several consecutive weeks show no movement and the notes column doesn't point to an obvious explanation, that's a reasonable point to review the broader picture, though nothing here should be read as an instruction for how any individual should adjust their course.

## A worked example across six weeks

Consider a log reading 84.0, 83.5, 83.6, 83.1, 83.1, 82.6kg across six weeks. Two of those weeks show essentially no change from the one before. Looked at as a full six-week block, though, the direction is clearly downward, roughly 1.4kg over six weeks, a completely normal and steady pace. Neither flat week on its own would have told the full story.

### Why isolated weeks feel more significant than they are

There's a natural tendency to weight the most recent entry more heavily than it deserves, especially when it's flat or slightly up. Deliberately looking at the last month as a block, rather than just the most recent one or two entries, corrects for this and gives a far more accurate read of what's actually happening.

## When a flat stretch coincides with a dose change

A flat week or two often lines up with a recent dose increase, as the body adjusts to a new level. Noting dose changes in the same log as the weight entries makes it easier to see this kind of coincidence when it happens, rather than treating a flat week during a dose transition as unexplained.

### A final practical note

A flat week deserves a shrug, not a spiral. The log will tell the real story once a few more weeks are added to it.

### Keeping perspective across the whole course

Zooming out to look at the full course so far, not just the last month, is a useful exercise whenever a flat stretch starts to feel discouraging. The wider view almost always tells a steadier, more reassuring story than the most recent few weeks alone.

### One last reminder

Patience with the process, backed by a log that shows the real picture over time, is generally more useful than judging any course by its single most recent entry.

## The bottom line

A single flat week is a normal, expected part of any weigh-in log and rarely signals anything on its own. Looking at the trend across several weeks, with notes providing context for any single unusual entry, gives a far more accurate picture than reacting to one week in isolation.
