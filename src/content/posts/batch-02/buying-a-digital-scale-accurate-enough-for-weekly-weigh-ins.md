## What "accurate enough" means for this purpose

For a weekly weigh-in log, a scale doesn't need laboratory-grade precision, it needs to be consistent with itself. A scale that reads within 0.1kg to 0.2kg on two back-to-back weighings, taken a minute apart, is accurate enough to track a real week-to-week trend. UK retailers sell basic digital scales meeting this bar from around £15 to £25.

### How to test a scale before trusting it

Step on, note the reading, step off, wait a few seconds, step back on. If the second reading matches the first within 0.1 to 0.2kg, the scale is reliable. Repeating this test two or three times over the first week of ownership, at different times of day, gives more confidence than a single check.

## What causes inaccurate readings

A scale on carpet or an uneven floor is the most common cause of unreliable readings, since the base can shift slightly and throw off the calibration. Old or low batteries can also cause drift on digital scales, so replacing batteries at the first sign of an inconsistent reading is worth trying before assuming the scale itself is faulty.

### Where to place it for the best readings

A hard, level floor, tile or laminate rather than carpet, in the same spot every time, gives the most consistent results. Moving the scale between rooms or floor types week to week introduces variation that has nothing to do with an actual weight change.

## Do more expensive scales mean more accurate readings

Beyond a certain point, roughly £25 to £30 in the UK market, extra spend tends to buy features like Bluetooth syncing or body composition estimates rather than meaningfully better weight accuracy. A £15 scale placed correctly and tested for consistency is just as trustworthy for weekly tracking as a £60 smart scale, for the core purpose of tracking weight.

### When it's worth spending more

Paying more makes sense for convenience (automatic app logging) or for genuinely wanting the extra composition readings, not for weight accuracy alone. Anyone buying purely to log weight week to week can reasonably stick with a basic model.

## Fitting an accurate scale into the log

Weighing at a similar time of day each week, most commonly first thing in the morning before eating or drinking, removes more variation from the trend than any amount of extra spend on the scale itself. The habit around the scale matters as much as the scale.

## Checking a scale's own calibration claims

UK regulations require scales sold for medical or trade use to meet specific calibration standards, but general consumer bathroom scales aren't held to the same requirement. Checking product reviews specifically for mentions of consistency, rather than relying on marketing claims about precision to a fraction of a gram, gives a more realistic sense of what a specific model actually delivers in practice.

### Recalibrating a scale that starts drifting

Some digital scales include a manual recalibration process, often involving a known reference weight, described in the manual. If a scale that was previously reliable starts giving inconsistent readings, checking whether it supports recalibration before assuming it needs replacing can save an unnecessary repurchase.

## Temperature and other environmental factors

Some digital scales can read slightly differently in a cold bathroom compared with a warm one, particularly older models with less temperature-compensated sensors. If a bathroom tends to vary significantly in temperature across the year, weighing in a consistently heated space, or simply being aware that a seasonal reading shift might be partly environmental rather than a real change, helps interpret the log more accurately.

### Keeping the scale itself well maintained

Wiping the scale's surface and base clean occasionally, and checking the battery compartment for any corrosion if the scale sits in a humid bathroom, helps it keep reading accurately over the length of a multi-month course rather than developing an unnoticed drift.

## A short buying checklist

A digital scale tested for consistency within 0.1 to 0.2kg on repeat readings, placed on a hard level floor in the same spot each time, batteries replaced at the first sign of drift, and a consistent weigh-in time chosen to keep the weekly trend meaningful.
