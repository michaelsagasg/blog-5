## Start with consistency, not features

The single requirement a weigh-in scale must meet is giving the same reading twice in a row. Before looking at price or extra features, this is the test that matters: step on, note the number, step off, step back on within a minute, and check the two readings match within 0.1 to 0.2kg. Basic UK digital scales from £15 to £25 generally pass this test easily.

### Where budget scales fall short

The cheapest scales, often unbranded and sold for under £10, are more likely to drift or give inconsistent readings, particularly on anything but a perfectly hard, flat floor. Spending slightly more, in the £15 to £25 range, generally buys a noticeably steadier scale.

## Placement matters as much as the scale itself

A hard, level floor, tile or laminate rather than carpet, gives far more consistent readings than a soft or uneven surface, regardless of how good the scale is. Keeping the scale in the same spot every week removes one more variable from the weekly comparison.

### Testing before the first real weigh-in

Running the consistency test described above two or three times in the first week, at different times of day, builds confidence in the scale before relying on it for a log that might run for months.

## Deciding on extra features

Bluetooth or Wi-Fi syncing to an app, priced from roughly £30 upwards in the UK, automatically records each weigh-in without manual entry. Body fat, muscle mass and water percentage readings, common on scales above £25, use bioelectrical impedance and are rough estimates that shift with hydration, more useful as a slow monthly trend than a weekly number.

### Is a smart scale worth it

A smart scale is a convenience for people who know a manual writing step is more likely to make a habit lapse. For anyone who's comfortable jotting a number down each week, a basic scale does exactly the same core job for a fraction of the price.

## Setting the routine around the scale

Weighing at a similar time each week, most commonly first thing in the morning before eating or drinking, reduces more variation in the weekly number than any upgrade to the scale itself. The habit built around the scale matters more than which model was bought.

## A quick comparison of typical UK price bands

Basic mechanical (non-digital) scales, around £10 to £15, are the cheapest option but generally less precise and harder to read consistently than a digital display. Standard digital scales, £15 to £25, are the sweet spot for most weekly logs: consistent, easy to read, no unnecessary extras. Scales with body composition and app syncing, £30 upwards, add convenience and extra metrics but don't meaningfully improve core weight accuracy over the standard digital band.

### Matching the scale to the actual goal

Anyone whose only goal is a reliable weekly weight number for a log doesn't need to spend above the standard digital band. The extra spend only pays off for someone who specifically wants automatic app logging or is genuinely interested in tracking body composition trends over the long term.

## Reading reviews with a critical eye

Star ratings alone don't say much about consistency, since most complaints about bathroom scales come from a small number of specific issues: drift over time, a wobbly base, or a display that's hard to read in low light. Searching review text for these specific words, rather than trusting the overall star average, gives a more useful picture of whether a particular model will hold up over months of weekly use.

### Buying two scales isn't necessary

Some people wonder whether buying two scales and averaging the readings improves accuracy. In practice, this adds complexity without meaningfully improving a weekly log, since a single well-placed, tested scale used consistently already removes most of the avoidable error. One reliable scale, used the same way every week, beats two scales used inconsistently.

## A short buying checklist

A scale tested for consistency within 0.1 to 0.2kg, placed on a hard level floor in a fixed spot, extra features (app sync, body composition) added only if genuinely wanted rather than assumed necessary, and a consistent weigh-in time chosen from week one.
