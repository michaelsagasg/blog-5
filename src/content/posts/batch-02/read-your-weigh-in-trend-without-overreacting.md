## Look at four weeks, not one

The single most useful habit for reading a weigh-in log is comparing a rolling block of four weeks against the previous four, rather than reacting to any single entry. A trend built from several weeks smooths out the water retention, digestion, and hormonal noise that makes any one week unreliable on its own.

### How to build this into a log

Adding a simple column or note every fourth week that averages the last four entries turns raw weekly numbers into a trend line that's far easier to read honestly. This takes a couple of minutes and makes the difference between a log that causes anxiety and one that actually shows progress.

## Expect at least one flat or up week in most months

Across any four-to-six week stretch, it's common to see at least one week with no change or a small increase, even when the overall direction is downward. This is normal variation, not a sign that something has stopped working, and treating it as a red flag is one of the most common reasons people lose confidence in a log that's actually going fine.

### What to check before reacting to a flat week

Reviewing the log's other entries for that week, sleep, stress, a heavier meal the night before, a different weigh-in time, often explains a flat or unusual week without needing to change anything about the underlying course.

## Separate the numbers from the story

A log entry is just a number and a few notes. The interpretation, whether that number means something has gone wrong, is a separate step that benefits from waiting until there's a clear four-week trend rather than reacting the same day a number looks off. Writing the number down calmly and reviewing it later, rather than judging it in the moment, is a habit worth building early.

### A simple rule of thumb

If the four-week trend is heading in the expected direction, individual weeks within it don't need individual explanations. If the four-week trend itself has flattened or reversed, that's the point worth looking at more closely, not any single week inside it.

## Using notes to add context, not excuses

A brief note next to an unusual week, "travelled this week, different scale," "higher sodium meals, expect some water retention," gives future review of the log useful context without turning every entry into a justification. This distinction, context versus excuse, keeps the log honest and useful.

## A simple way to visualise the trend

Even without any special software, plotting weekly numbers on a basic hand-drawn or spreadsheet line chart makes a trend far easier to read than a column of numbers alone. A line that's generally sloping in the expected direction, even with visible bumps along the way, is much easier to interpret calmly than scanning a list of numbers and mentally comparing each one to the last.

### Reviewing the chart, not just the numbers

Setting aside a few minutes every four weeks to actually look at the chart, rather than just adding new numbers to it, turns the log into a genuine review tool rather than a running list that never gets properly read back.

## When outside perspective helps

If a trend genuinely feels confusing or concerning even after reviewing it over several weeks, showing the log, or a simple chart built from it, to a pharmacist, GP, or another trusted source of guidance is a reasonable step. A visual record makes this kind of conversation far more concrete than trying to describe a vague impression of "it doesn't seem to be working" from memory alone.

### A final practical note

The goal of a well-read log is calm, not certainty. A trend can be read sensibly without needing to explain every single bump along the way.

### One last reminder

Progress on a GLP-1 course rarely looks like a perfectly straight line, and a log built to read the trend rather than the noise reflects that reality far more accurately.

## The bottom line

Reading a weigh-in trend well means looking at four-week blocks rather than single entries, expecting some weeks to be flat or up even during genuine progress, and using log notes to add context rather than react to noise. This approach keeps a log a useful tool rather than a source of weekly anxiety.
