## The mg content, backed by a certificate of analysis

A listing should state the vial's mg content clearly, supported by a batch-specific certificate of analysis. This single figure underpins every calculation a log will eventually depend on, from reconstitution maths to the units drawn each week, so a listing without it leaves a first-time buyer working from an unverified number.

### Why this matters more for a first order

A first-time buyer starting a log has nothing to compare a new listing against yet. A COA-backed mg figure, clearly shown rather than buried in fine print, removes one major source of uncertainty right from the first order, before any log entries exist to sanity-check it against.

## Reconstitution guidance, if the product needs it

A listing that suggests a water volume and the resulting concentration, clearly separated from the COA-verified mg content, makes the first few log entries considerably easier to set up correctly. Without this, a first-time buyer is left doing the calculation from scratch, with more room for error on the very first dose.

### What good guidance looks like

Clear guidance states something like "reconstitute with X ml of bacteriostatic water for a concentration of Y mg per ml," in plain terms, rather than leaving a buyer to infer it from the mg content alone.

## Dispatch and realistic delivery detail

Same-day dispatch cutoffs and honest UK delivery estimates let a buyer plan exactly when a first dose, and the first log entry, will actually happen. A listing vague about timing makes it harder to plan the first week of a log around a specific start date.

## Supplies included or not

A listing explicit about whether syringes, alcohol swabs or bacteriostatic water are included avoids a first-time buyer being caught short on injection day, having assumed something was in the box that wasn't. This detail, small as it seems, is one of the most common gaps in listings that otherwise look thorough.

### A general dosing schedule, where relevant

Some listings, Enzo Meds among them, include a general weekly dosing schedule showing how a typical titration might progress. This isn't personalised medical guidance, but it gives a first-time logger a reference point for setting up the log's structure before the first dose arrives.

## The overall picture

A listing with COA-backed mg content, clear reconstitution guidance, realistic UK delivery information, explicit clarity on what's included, and ideally a general dosing schedule, gives a first-time logger everything needed to start a log accurately from week one rather than guessing at half the details.

## Red flags worth watching for

A listing with no visible COA, vague or contradictory mg claims across different parts of the page, or pricing dramatically lower than every comparable listing is worth extra scrutiny before ordering. None of these alone is necessarily a dealbreaker, but two or more together are a reasonable signal to look elsewhere or ask direct questions before committing.

### Customer service as an informal check

Sending a simple pre-order question, about the COA, reconstitution guidance, or delivery timing, and seeing how quickly and clearly a seller responds is a useful, low-cost way to judge a listing beyond what's written on the page itself. A seller that answers plainly within a day or two is generally a better sign than one that's slow or evasive.

## Saving listing details for later reference

Screenshotting or saving a copy of the listing's key details, mg content, COA, reconstitution guidance, at the time of ordering is worth doing, since listings can be updated or changed after the fact. Having a saved record of exactly what was shown at the point of purchase is useful if any detail needs to be checked again weeks or months later.

### A final practical note

A thorough listing takes only a few extra minutes to read properly, and that small time investment upfront saves considerably more time and confusion once the order actually arrives.

## A short checklist before ordering

COA shown and current for the specific batch. Reconstitution guidance stated in plain terms if the product needs it. Dispatch and delivery timing given honestly. Included supplies listed explicitly. A general schedule shown, if the seller provides one, as a planning reference rather than a fixed instruction.
