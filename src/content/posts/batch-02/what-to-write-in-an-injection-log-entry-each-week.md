## The four core fields

A complete weekly entry needs four things at minimum: the date, the dose given (in mg or units, depending on the format used), the injection site, and the current weight. These four fields, written consistently every week, form the backbone of a useful log and are enough on their own to spot a trend over time.

### Why site matters as much as dose

Recording the injection site each week, not just the dose, is what makes injection site rotation something that can actually be checked rather than assumed. Without this field, it's easy to unintentionally favour the same spot week after week.

## Optional fields worth adding

Beyond the core four, a notes column covering side effects, appetite changes, sleep quality, or anything unusual that week turns a bare number log into a record that explains itself later. A brief note like "mild nausea day two, gone by day four" is more useful six months later than trying to recall it from memory.

### Keeping notes brief and consistent

A short phrase is more sustainable to write every week than a full paragraph. The goal is enough context to jog memory later, not a detailed essay, since an overly demanding format is one of the most common reasons a log gets abandoned after a few weeks.

## What to write immediately versus later

Writing the dose and site down immediately after the injection, while it's fresh, avoids relying on memory later in the day. The weight entry can be added at the same time if weigh-in day coincides with injection day, or added separately if the routine is split across different days.

### A simple order that works

Draw or dial the dose, inject, write the entry, dispose of the needle. Doing the log entry as the second-to-last step, right after the injection and before disposal, means it never gets skipped or forgotten once the sharps bin is closed.

## Reviewing entries later

A log is only as useful as it is readable later. Keeping the format consistent week to week, same fields, same order, makes it far easier to scan back through months of entries and spot a pattern than a log where the format shifted partway through.

## An example of a complete entry

"Week 6, Tuesday 14 October. Dose: 4mg (0.4ml drawn from 10mg/ml vial). Site: left thigh, outer. Weight: 81.3kg. Notes: mild nausea for two days after dose, gone by Thursday, otherwise normal week." This single entry, written in under a minute, captures everything needed to understand that week months later without relying on memory.

### What to leave out

A log entry doesn't need unrelated daily details, general mood, unrelated errands, work stress, unless directly relevant to understanding a dose or a weight reading. Keeping entries focused on the fields that matter for the log's purpose keeps it quick to write and quick to review later, rather than turning into a general diary that's harder to scan for patterns.

## Adjusting the template as the course progresses

A template that starts simple, the core four fields, can gain an extra column or two as a course goes on, once the basic habit is solid. Adding injection site rotation tracking or a monthly measurement row after the first month, rather than trying to include everything from week one, is a reasonable way to build up a more detailed log without overwhelming the habit before it's established.

### A final practical note

A log that's easy to write is a log that gets written. Keeping the weekly effort small is what actually makes months of consistent entries possible.

### Making the fields feel automatic

After a few weeks, filling in the same five fields stops feeling like a task and starts feeling like simply finishing the injection routine. That shift, from effort to habit, is the real goal of keeping the template consistent from the very first week.

### One last reminder

Even an imperfect entry written quickly is more valuable than a perfect one that never gets written because it felt like too much effort.

## A short template

Date. Dose (mg or units). Injection site. Weight. Notes (side effects, appetite, anything unusual). Five lines, written the same way every week, is enough to build a log that's genuinely useful to look back on months into a course.
