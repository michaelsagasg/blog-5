## A printed body diagram is the simplest tool

A basic printed diagram showing the abdomen, thighs and upper arms, the common subcutaneous injection zones, lets a weekly log mark which spot was used with a simple tick or number. These diagrams are free to print from countless templates online, or come included in some bought injection log journals, typically £8 to £15 if buying a dedicated one.

### Why site rotation is worth tracking

Repeatedly injecting the same small spot can lead to skin changes at that site over time, so rotating between different areas and different points within each area is commonly recommended. A visual record makes it far easier to see at a glance which spots have been used recently and which haven't, compared with trying to remember from week to week.

## A simple rotation schedule

Some people buy or print a rotation chart that divides each zone into a grid, working through each square in sequence before repeating. Others simply alternate between left and right sides of the abdomen and thighs on a loose rotation. Either method works, and the "supply" needed is really just a printed diagram and a pen, costing next to nothing.

### Apps that track this automatically

Some injection tracking apps include a body map feature, letting a site be tapped on a diagram and logged automatically with the date. This removes the need for a separate paper chart, though it ties the record to a specific app rather than a portable page that can be photographed or filed away.

## What else helps

A body marker or a very light, washable skin pen, sold cheaply online or in craft shops, lets some people mark the general area used directly on the skin as a same-day reminder, wiped off before the next shower. This isn't necessary for everyone but can help in the first few weeks before a mental rotation routine feels automatic.

### Keeping the chart with the rest of the log

Storing the rotation chart in the same place as the weekly log, whether that's the same notebook page or the same folder, means checking it takes no extra effort as part of the existing weekly routine, rather than becoming a separate task that gets skipped.

## A simple DIY chart if buying isn't necessary

A rotation chart doesn't need to be bought or even printed from a template. Drawing two simple outlines, a front view of the abdomen and thighs, divided into a grid of six to eight boxes each, takes a few minutes on a plain sheet of paper and does exactly the same job as a bought or printed version, for free.

### Numbering the grid for a simple rotation order

Numbering each box one through however many zones are marked, then working through them in order before starting again, removes any need to remember which spot was used last. Ticking off the number in the weekly log alongside the date is enough to keep the whole system running with minimal effort.

## Combining a chart with an app

For anyone using a digital tracking app that includes a body map feature, using it alongside a simple printed backup isn't redundant, it's a reasonable safeguard in case the app is ever unavailable, lost, or discontinued, keeping the rotation record intact regardless of what happens to any single device or piece of software.

### A final practical note

The specific tool matters far less than the habit of actually checking it every week. A free paper chart used consistently beats an elaborate bought one that gets ignored after the first month.

### One last reminder

Rotation is one of the easiest good habits to build into a log from the very first week, since it costs nothing beyond a small amount of attention and a place to write it down.

## A short buying checklist

A printed or bought body diagram covering the main injection zones, a pen kept with the log for marking it, and optionally a tracking app with a built-in body map if a fully digital record is preferred. None of this needs to cost more than the price of paper and ink if a free template is used instead of a bought journal.
